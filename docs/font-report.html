The ranking is a measurement, not a comfort guarantee. No study ranks typefaces by eye strain, and this one does not either. What is measured here is how far apart the ambiguous glyphs are at reading size, how much ink a screenful of code lays down, how much room a typeface gives small text, and which weight suits each polarity. Those are proxies for decoding effort.

Polarity is a bigger lever than typeface: Dobres et al. (2016) measured ~38.6% lower duration thresholds for black-on-white than white-on-black, and ~8.8% for a humanist over a square grotesque at the same cap-height. In the same study there was no typeface × polarity interaction — one ranking of faces holds for both modes, which is why this document ranks once and recommends a weight per polarity only as convention. The polarity gap widens as text shrinks (Piepenbrock et al. 2013, cited in both reviews), so the order of levers is size first, then polarity, then typeface.

Read this before the leaderboard. No study in the reviewed literature ranks coding fonts by eye strain, error rate or session length. What is measured here are the properties the literature does support: x-height and aperture, stroke weight and modulation, spacing, cell geometry, and how far apart the glyphs that get confused actually are. Nahar et al. (2007) did find a typeface effect on reported visual discomfort (p = 0.039) across 31 observers, so the face is not nothing — but a p-value on discomfort across unnamed faces is not a ranking you can read off. Age, uncorrected refractive error, room brightness and font size are all larger levers than the family you pick. Pick one of the top several, set the size properly, and stop optimising.

Which weight, and for which polarity

This section is convention, not evidence. The claim that dark mode wants a heavier stroke than light mode is repeated widely in design guidance, and the reviewed literature does not establish it: the bloom or halation mechanism is not even named in the two most relevant reviews, and Dobres et al. (2016) found no typeface × polarity interaction at all — one ranking of faces held in both polarities. What follows is therefore a recommendation you are free to ignore, sitting on top of a measurement you are not: the stem width of every measured weight, per family.

The declared bands are 0.070–0.090 em of stem for light mode and 0.090–0.112 em for dark mode, and the recommendation is the measured weight whose measured stem lands nearest the middle of the band. Where a family has no weight in the band, the nearest weight is named and flagged — a two-weight family has only Regular and Bold to offer, and that jump is usually larger than the band asked for.

Reading the shape: a wide, round polygon is a face that gives up nothing, but the axes are not equally load-bearing. Glyph distinction saturates at 100 for every design in the top eight — among the coding fonts measured here it is a solved problem, and on this evidence it separates only the legacy faces, so use it as a filter rather than as a ranking. X-height carries the most weight after it, and density, spacing and monolinearity are bands: the middle is best and both ends are worse, so a font can lose points for being too light as well as too heavy.

Weight range is the axis most often overlooked. A family that ships one weight cannot be tuned at all, and it is the axis on which the otherwise excellent Hack build here loses the most.

Method, and what this does not measure

  • What is measured. Every number comes from font binaries on this machine, installed or downloaded for the post-2019 test, read with fontTools and rasterised with FreeType through Pillow. Shape measurements are taken at 192 px per em so a one-pixel quantisation is under 2% of a stem; density is measured at 13 px because that is the size a terminal body text actually occupies. Hinting is whatever the shipped file carries and whatever FreeType does with it.
  • Distinction is the symmetric difference of two glyph masks over their union, aligned on the pen origin so advance and side-bearing differences count, measured for 34 pairs at design size and again at 13 px. An area distance under-weights a small but decisive feature — a dotted zero scores as 'similar to O' however well it reads — so a pair whose shapes measure close is lifted to a declared floor when an explicit cue keeps it apart, and the lift is shown in the ambiguity table. The axis is scored by subtraction: no marked zero, fewer than two cues across the 1/l/I set, and each pair below 0.20 that nothing disambiguates. Any pair at all below 0.15 is a fault, not a preference.
  • Cues are measured, not read from documentation. A flag is ink to the left of the stem in the upper third; an arm is ink to the right of it; a foot is the stem's centre of mass drifting sideways in the bottom twelfth; a base is ink at the foot wider than the stem; serifs are ink wider than the stem at both the top and the bottom rows of the capital eye; a zero cue is ink inside the counter, classified as a dot or a slash by whether its row centres drift as it descends. Two members of the 1/l/I set count as resolved only when each carries a cue and the two cue sets differ — an outline that merely happens to measure different is not a deliberate disambiguation.
  • Optical density is ink pixels divided by the cell area those glyphs occupied (advance × line height) for a specimen of code and prose at 13 px. It is a proxy for how dark a screenful of text looks, which is the quantity that decides whether a face reads as faint or as clogged.
  • x-height and line spacing come from the OS/2 table where it is filled in and from outline bounds where it is not. Stroke contrast is measured from a rasterised ‘o’ at 192 px: the left and right walls at mid-height are the thick strokes, the top and bottom at mid-width are the thin ones, and the reported ratio is their quotient. 1.00 is monolinear.
  • Terminal furniture is whether a rule glyph reaches both cell edges with no interior gap, measured by projection over two cells rather than by sampling one row, plus which of the glyph groups a TUI draws are present at all.
  • Weight range counts the upright weights measured and whether the family is variable. It is a capability: more weights mean you can tune stem weight without changing typeface. The polarity table is separate and is convention, not evidence — see below.
  • One row per design. A Nerd-Font-patched build, a Mono or Propo variant and a relabelled reissue carry the same letterforms, so they merge into one row when their rasterised glyphs hash the same, or when their names normalise to the same design and their outline signatures agree within one percent of contour area, allowing two glyphs of drift for an earlier version of the same design. The aliases are listed on each card. When upstream variants differ only in which OpenType features are on by default — Iosevka Fixed and Iosevka Term against Iosevka, JetBrains Mono NL against JetBrains Mono — the measured outlines, ASCII advances and rule-glyph advances are identical and the measurement cannot tell them apart. Those merge, and the card names every family that went into the row so the choice is visible.
  • Fonts under test. Downloaded fonts are measured the same way and read on the installed fonts' scale, so testing one cannot change another's score. A download of a design already installed is not scored: the installed copy keeps its numbers. All faces downloaded for one catalogue entry form one row under the catalogue's name. A variable-only family gets one static file per named instance, because a variable file measures at its default, and several default to Thin or ExtraLight. Installed variable fonts are not expanded this way, so their weight axis counts one weight plus the variable credit.
  • Two weightings, one measurement set. Long-session weights distinction .22, x-height .16, density .14, weight range .12, spacing .10, monolinearity .10, furniture .10, rendering .06. Density-first weights columns per screen .28, x-height .18, density .16, distinction .14, spacing .08, monolinearity .06, weights .06, rendering .02, furniture .02. The columns-per-screen axis is a real trade-off, not a free win: condensed widths measurably impair letter recognition, so the density-first view is buying screen space at a legibility cost.
  • Polarity weight bands are convention. The claim that dark mode wants a heavier stroke than light mode is repeated in design guidance and is not established by any trial in the reviewed set: the bloom or halation mechanism is not even named in the two most relevant reviews, and Dobres et al. found no typeface × polarity interaction. The bands 0.070–0.090 em (light) and 0.090–0.112 em (dark) are a declared convention. The measurement underneath them — the stem width of every installed weight — is real, and is printed in full on every card so you can ignore the convention and choose from the data.
  • Precision. Ratios and distances are quoted to three or four decimals because that is what the rasteriser produces, not because the third decimal means anything. Treat differences under about three points on the hundred-point scales as noise, and the ordering of adjacent rows as a coin toss.
  • Not measured: eye strain, blink rate, accommodative response, critical fusion frequency, error rate, reading speed, or performance over a session. Critical flicker fusion is a known dead end for this — Singh et al. (2023) found it uncorrelated with reported fatigue after a two-hour task. Also not measured: how conventional a design looks, which is a real determinant of reading speed for an experienced reader and which no axis here captures; your own corrected acuity, viewing distance and monitor; the rendering stack as a whole; the palettes the text sits on, which is the sibling report's subject; and coding fonts neither installed here nor in the post-2019 test, including older catalogue fonts and the commercial ones, whose files were not measured.
  • Sizing beats choosing. The one practical finding worth acting on is that smaller text is worse: font size is a digital-eye-strain risk factor, and Nahar et al. found small font size increased reported discomfort. If a face wins here by being narrow, the columns you gain are paid for in recognition accuracy. Go up a point before you go shopping.

Bands and anchors

Every axis is a scale or a band. A declared anchor is placed by hand because the literature gives the direction but not the number; an observed band comes from the 5th–95th percentile of the installed fonts, so it ranks against that set rather than certifying anything, and a font under test cannot move the scale it is read on.

  • declared x-height anchors 0.46 em → 0 and 0.56 em → 100; measured spread was 0.419–0.547 em
  • declared stroke-contrast anchors 1.35:1 → 0 and 1.00:1 → 100; measured spread was 1.00–1.28:1
  • declared distinction is scored by subtraction, not by a scale: −15 when the zero carries no mark, −15 when the 1/l/I set carries fewer than two cues between them, −15 for each pair on the code-critical list and −10 for each other pair measuring below 0.20 that nothing resolves. Closest measured pairs over the installed set run 0.217 to 0.392.

  • band ink density 0.0809–0.0881 of the cell scores 100, falling to 0 at 0.0473 and 0.1020
  • band default line height 1.16–1.25 em scores 100, falling to 0 at 0.95 and 1.91 em

  • Beier, Oderkerk, Bay, Larsen, Liu, Berkner, Klette, Sawyer, dos Santos, Warriner, Watson, Wright, Zhu & Dobres (2022), Foundations and Trends in Human–Computer Interaction 16(4):214–324 — review: open letterforms, larger counters, non-condensed widths and generous spacing improve small-size and glance legibility; very heavy or very light weights impair recognition; small-size cuts historically use lower stroke contrast. No numeric optimum for x-height is given. pdf
  • Nahar, Sheedy, Hayes & Tai (2007), Optometry and Vision Science 84(8):728–735 — 31 observers, graded size, typeface, contrast, refractive error and glare: all conditions raised visual discomfort, typeface at p = 0.039; small font and low contrast did not raise orbicularis EMG and decreased blink rate. pubmed
  • Singh, Downie & Anderson (2023) — after a two-hour demanding computer task, visual-fatigue questionnaire scores rose while critical flicker fusion did not, with no significant correlation: CFF is not a usable surrogate for visual-fatigue symptoms. pubmed
  • Piepenbrock, Mayr & Buchner (2013) — polarity differences grow as text size decreases, cited in both reviews above. Negative polarity is generally less legible despite emitting less light.
  • Daxer et al. (2022) — no significant reading-speed difference between a serif and a sans on paper with conditions equated, which undercuts blanket ‘this face is more readable’ claims in either direction. pmc
  • Butterick, Practical Typography, line length — 45–90 characters per line as prose guidance, which caps how much of a wide monitor is usefully filled. away
  • Rougier (2020), On the design of text editors, arXiv:2008.06030 — discusses editor typography and explicitly declines to prescribe values for want of a user study. arxiv
  • Unicode UTR #36, Unicode Security Considerations — visual spoofing and homoglyphs, the usual citation for 0/O and 1/l/I being separately encoded but often alike. It is a security report, not a reading experiment. unicode.org

These are design assertions written by designers; no trial in the reviewed set measures a ligature or a dotted zero against comprehension, reading time or error rate. They are cited here as intent, not as evidence.
